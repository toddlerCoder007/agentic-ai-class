Start with the whys

In Lesson 1 you learned to turn a request back into a problem. But the first problem you hear is usually a symptom. This lesson teaches the Five Whys, which takes you from the symptom to a cause you can actually fix.

Ask why, five times

Source: IDG, Module 2: Start With The Whys.

IDG presents the Five Whys as a simple tool for understanding why a problem exists before you reach for a solution. Start with the symptom and ask “why?” Then ask “why?” about the answer. Each answer peels back one layer.

Here is IDG’s government example, in which applicants can’t get the grants they applied for:

Paraphrased from IDG’s “Five Whys: two examples” graphic. IDG’s other example is everyday life: you ran a red light, and the root cause turns out to be an alarm-clock battery you forgot to replace.
  1. SymptomApplicants can’t get the grants they applied for.
  2. Why?Applications take too long to process.
  3. Why?Officers spend a lot of time going back and forth with applicants.
  4. Why?Many applications arrive incomplete.
  5. Why?The form is full of internal jargon.
  6. Why?The form was designed around how the agency works, not how applicants think.
  7. So the fix is to redesign the form, not the processing system.

Look at what happens if you stop at the second line. “Processing is slow” invites a new processing system: expensive, slow to build, and it would not stop incomplete forms arriving. The fifth answer points to something much cheaper that fixes the real problem.

Choosing the level to fix

IDG adds a warning: problems rarely have a single root cause. The aim is not to find the one perfect root cause. It is to find a cause that you can control and that is closely connected to the outcome you want.

From IDG’s “Choosing the right level to fix” graphic.
AvoidAim for
Fixing a symptom while the root cause is still there.A cause close to the root.
Causes outside your sphere of influence.A cause you can act on within your timeline and resources.

The level you fix at depends on who you are. A cause that one team can’t touch might be the right place for another team to work.

Practice 3 · Your own problem

Run the Five Whys on something real

This is IDG’s own exercise. Pick a problem your division is facing right now. Ask “why?” five times and write each answer. Then tick the answers that sit within your sphere of influence.

    Recall it

    Answer from memory first, then open each one to check. The last question goes back to Lesson 1. Revisiting earlier material like this helps you remember it.

    What two kinds of cause does IDG say to avoid fixing?

    Symptoms, where the root cause would still be there afterwards, and causes outside your sphere of influence.

    In IDG’s grants example, why is “build a faster processing system” the wrong fix?

    Slow processing is a symptom. The cause is a form full of jargon, designed around the agency rather than the applicant. Redesigning the form is cheaper, and it stops incomplete applications arriving in the first place.

    From Lesson 1: what three questions does a problem statement answer?

    Who are our users? What are they trying to do? What’s getting in their way?

    Bring your chain to the next session Keep the Five Whys you wrote in Practice 3. In Lesson 3 it becomes the Cause part of a full problem statement.
