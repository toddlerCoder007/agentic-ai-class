Solutions in disguise

Most requests at work arrive as a solution: “we need a chatbot”. Your first move as a product thinker is to turn the request back into the problem behind it. This lesson teaches that move.

Why start here

Source: IDG, Module 1: Understanding the Problem.
“Great digital products start with problems, not solutions.” Institute of Digital Government, Product Thinking, Module 1

IDG’s point is that a project can be well run and still fail, because it was solving the wrong problem. The team ships on time and on budget, and nobody’s life gets better.

The usual cause is that the project started from a solution. IDG lists requests it hears all the time. Each one sounds like a problem but actually names something to build or buy:

These four pairs are paraphrased from IDG’s “common trap” graphic in Module 1.
What we hearThe problem underneath
We need more AI.Officers can’t make decisions from large, messy datasets.
We need a superapp.Citizens can’t get to services efficiently across many platforms.
We don’t have a CRM.Clunky processes cause long waits for citizens.
Our product is end of life.Citizens and officers are about to lose access to critical services.

The right-hand column can be solved in many ways. The left-hand column has already picked one, often before anyone checked whether it fits.

The test: three questions

IDG’s first principle is to focus on the problem, not the solution. It comes with three questions. A real problem statement answers all three:

  1. Who are our users?
  2. What are they trying to do?
  3. What’s getting in their way?
IDG’s other two principles, “chase outcomes, not outputs” and “integrate policy, ops and tech”, are covered in later lessons.

A solution in disguise skips all three and names a thing instead: an app, a bot, a system or a migration. A tell-tale sign is that you could delete the people from the sentence and it would still make sense.

The test does not ban a statement that mentions users. “Build an app so citizens can pay fines” has users in it, but it still decides what to build before saying what is getting in the citizens’ way.

IDG cites Grab (safer, easier taxi booking), Amazon (books only) and Netflix (cheaper, more reliable DVD rentals) as companies that began this way.

A good problem is also narrow. IDG points to companies that started by solving one specific problem for one defined group, and expanded only after that worked.

Practice 2 · Reframe it

Turn a request into a problem

Your director says: “We need a WhatsApp bot so residents can book clinic appointments.” Pretend you have asked around, and found out the clinic phone line is only staffed 9 to 5. Most callers are working adults, and they can’t get through during office hours.

Answer the three questions. Don’t name anything to build.

    Show one good answer

    Who: working-age residents who need a clinic appointment.
    Trying to: book a slot that fits around their working hours.
    In the way: they can only book by phone, the line is staffed 9 to 5, and those are the hours they’re at work.

    Notice that a WhatsApp bot is one fix. Longer phone hours, online booking, or walk-in evening slots would also address it. The problem statement leaves those options open. The original request ruled them out.

    Recall it

    Without scrolling up, answer this in your head or on paper. Then check.

    What are the three questions a problem statement must answer?

    Who are our users? What are they trying to do? What’s getting in their way?

    What is the giveaway that a statement is a solution in disguise?

    It names a thing to build or buy (an app, AI, a system) before it says who is struggling and why. Often you can remove the people from the sentence and it still reads fine.

    Try it at work this week Next time someone brings you a request phrased as a solution, ask the three questions before discussing the solution. Write down what you hear and bring it to your next session. We can turn it into a full problem statement together.
    Stuck or curious? Ask your teacher. Bring any follow-up question to Claude in this workspace. For example: “Is ‘we need to digitalise this form’ a solution in disguise?” or “How do I push back on my director without sounding difficult?”
