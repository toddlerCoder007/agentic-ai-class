The 4Cs problem statement

You can now turn a request into a problem (Lesson 1) and dig down to a cause you can fix (Lesson 2). This lesson puts both together in a single statement that your team can agree on before anyone builds anything.

Four dimensions, one statement

Source: IDG, Module 3: Craft a Clear Problem Statement. The guiding questions are paraphrased from IDG’s 4Cs graphic.

IDG’s 4Cs framework comes after you have found the root cause. Its job is to state the problem clearly enough to keep the whole team aligned on it. IDG’s tagline for it: four dimensions, one statement, no solutions yet.

Clarity

Who is affected? What are they trying to do? What is broken, and how badly?

Consequence

What happens if nobody solves this? If the honest answer is “not much”, it may not be worth solving.

Cause

Why does the problem exist? This links back to your Five Whys and points toward the root.

Confirmation

What evidence shows the problem is real, significant and worth prioritising?

Notice that Clarity is Lesson 1’s three questions plus one more: how badly. And Cause is where your Five Whys chain ends up.

IDG’s worked example

Figures from IDG’s worked example: repeated form-filling across government services.
ClarityCitizens fill in the same information 15 or more times. Each form takes 20 to 30 minutes, and many give up and miss benefits they are entitled to.
ConsequencePeople lose support they qualify for, and trust erodes. The burden falls hardest on those least able to navigate complex processes.
CauseDifferent agencies built their services separately, at different times, with no shared data layer.
Confirmation68% of transactions are abandoned because of form-filling frustration: 204,000 out of 300,000 a year.

The statement never says “build a single sign-on” or “use AI to pre-fill forms”. Those might come later. For now the team only agrees on what is wrong, how much it matters, why it happens, and how they know.

Practice 2 · Write one

Draft a 4Cs statement

Back to the clinic from Lesson 1. Your director wanted a WhatsApp booking bot, and you reframed that as a problem. Since then, your team has collected the facts below. Write the 4Cs from them.

Fact sheet (made-up practice data)
  • Appointments can only be booked by phone. The line is staffed 9am to 5pm on weekdays.
  • The line gets 11,000 calls a month. 4,100 callers hang up after waiting more than 10 minutes.
  • 62% of callers are aged 25 to 59, and most call during their lunch break.
  • In a follow-up survey, 30% of callers who gave up didn’t book anywhere within two weeks. Some went to an emergency department instead.
  • Your Five Whys ended here: booking was set up when the phone was the only channel, and the line’s hours were set to match clinic opening hours.
    Compare with one strong answer

    Clarity: Working-age residents who need a clinic appointment can only book by phone, between 9am and 5pm on weekdays, which are their working hours. About 4,100 calls a month are abandoned after a wait of more than 10 minutes.

    Consequence: People put off care. Nearly a third of those who give up don’t book anywhere within two weeks, and some end up in emergency departments with conditions a clinic could treat.

    Cause: Booking was set up when the phone was the only channel. The line’s hours follow clinic opening hours, not the times when callers are free.

    Confirmation: Call logs show 4,100 of 11,000 monthly calls abandoned. 62% of callers are aged 25 to 59, and 30% of those who give up don’t book within two weeks.

    IDG’s final check: does the statement describe the problem, or does it sneak in a solution? This one leaves room for a bot, longer phone hours, online booking or evening walk-ins. The original request had already chosen one of them.

    Recall it

    Answer from memory first, then open each one to check. Two of the questions go back to earlier lessons.

    Name the 4Cs, and the question each one answers.

    Clarity: who, doing what, what’s broken, how badly. Consequence: what happens if it isn’t solved. Cause: why it exists. Confirmation: what evidence shows it’s real and worth prioritising.

    Which C can tell you a problem isn’t worth solving?

    Consequence. If not much happens when the problem goes unsolved, it may not deserve the team’s time.

    From Lesson 2: where does the Cause in a 4Cs statement come from?

    From your Five Whys: the deepest cause that is close to the root and within your sphere of influence.

    From Lesson 1: what gives away a solution in disguise?

    It names a thing to build or buy before saying who is struggling and why.

    Try it at work this week Take the Five Whys chain from Lesson 2’s Practice 3 and turn it into a 4Cs statement about your own problem. Then show it to a colleague and ask: “Do you agree this is the problem?” Their pushback is part of the learning. Bring it to your next session.
    Stuck or curious? Ask your teacher. Bring follow-up questions to Claude in this workspace. For example: “I don’t have any data for Confirmation yet. What counts as evidence?” or “Can you critique my 4Cs statement?”
